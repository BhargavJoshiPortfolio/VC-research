How each company in this portfolio is researched and judged. The aim is for every thesis to be comparable, evidence-based and honest about what a desk-based review can and cannot establish.

## 1. Why this is not classic equity research

Public-market equity research values a business from reported financials: earnings, cash flow, multiples. At pre-seed none of that exists. A company may have no revenue, a prototype, and a team of three. So the question changes.

| Public equity research | Pre-seed VC research |
|---|---|
| What is the business worth on current earnings? | Could this become a very large business, and what has to be true for that? |
| Anchored on financial statements | Anchored on team, insight, technology and early evidence |
| Diversified, steady returns | Power-law returns: a few winners pay for the rest |
| Downside measured by earnings misses | Downside is usually total loss, so the question is *why this one could be an outlier* |
| Valuation from multiples | Valuation from ownership, dilution and exit size needed |

Consequence: the output is not a price target. It is a reasoned view on **(a) the size of the outcome if it works, (b) the probability it works, and (c) whether the entry price and terms leave room for a fund-returning result.**

## 2. The return logic a VC is really testing

Every thesis should state the return math explicitly, because it explains why VCs pass on good but small businesses.

**Illustrative example (not a forecast).** A fund of €100M wants to return at least 3x, so €300M. One pre-seed cheque buys about 15% of a company. After three or four later rounds, dilution typically cuts that to roughly 7 to 8%. At 7.5% ownership, a €100M fund-return on a single company needs an exit near €1.3B. So the thesis must show a credible path to a billion-euro outcome, or to a smaller exit with a very low entry price.

For each company the thesis answers:
1. What is the plausible large outcome, and who would buy it or what would it take to IPO?
2. What ownership would an investor realistically hold at exit?
3. What probability would I assign to getting there? (Pre-seed success rates are low; say so.)

## 3. The dimensions that matter, in order of weight

At pre-seed, the most weight sits on things that are hardest to measure. The framework below keeps judgement structured.

### 3.1 Team (about 25% of the judgement)
The strongest predictor at this stage, and the hardest to verify from outside.
- **Founder-market fit:** why these people, for this problem? Domain depth, unfair access, lived experience.
- **Track record:** prior exits, technical credentials, relevant roles. Verify on LinkedIn, publications, patents, company registries.
- **Completeness:** is the technical, commercial and operating side covered? Name the missing key hire.
- **Speed and signal:** rate of shipping, hiring quality, how investors and advisers describe them.
- **Cohesion:** how long have the founders worked together? Equity split, roles.
- **Red flags:** single founder in a hard problem, no technical founder in a technical product, frequent changes of direction.

### 3.2 Problem and insight (about 20%)
- Is the problem painful, frequent and paid for today (or provably so soon)?
- What is the **non-obvious insight** that others miss? A good thesis can state it in one sentence.
- Why **now**? Regulation (NIS2, EU deforestation rules), technology shifts, cost curves, supply-chain sovereignty, labour shortage.
- Who is the buyer, who is the user, and are they the same person?

### 3.3 Product and technology defensibility (about 20%)
- What exists today: idea, prototype, pilot, product in market?
- **Moat candidates:** proprietary data, patents and freedom to operate, process know-how, regulatory approval, network effects, switching costs, scale economics, exclusive access to research or facilities.
- Honest test: *if a well-funded competitor started today, how long would it take to catch up?*
- Technical risk versus market risk. Deep-tech and biotech carry technical risk; software carries market and competition risk. Never treat them the same way.
- For claims like "10x more efficient", record them as **company claims** and look for independent validation (papers, trials, third-party customers).

### 3.4 Market (about 10%, but a gating factor)
- Build **bottom-up**: number of buyers x realistic price x achievable penetration. Treat top-down "€1.2 trillion" figures as marketing unless sourced.
- Identify the **wedge**: the narrow first use case that can win, and the path to expand from it.
- Size both the beachhead and the eventual market. A VC needs the second to be large.

### 3.5 Evidence and traction (about 15%)
Judge relative to stage. For pre-seed, strong evidence includes:
- Paying customers or paid pilots (better than unpaid pilots, better than letters of intent).
- Usage depth (daily active use, retention) rather than sign-ups.
- Named reference customers and strategic investors who are also customers.
- For science companies: data (preclinical results, greenhouse trials, wafer yields, demonstrations).
- Milestone evidence: grants won, validation agreements, accelerator selection (NATO DIANA, SPRIND, EIC).

### 3.6 Business model and path to scale (about 10%)
- How the company makes money, and where margins land at scale.
- Unit economics where knowable: gross margin, payback, take rate, utilisation, hardware cost per unit.
- Capital intensity: asset-light software versus pilot lines, fabs, fleets or clinical trials.
- Sales cycle length and who signs (airlines, farms, utilities and hospitals sell slowly).

### 3.7 Financing path and terms (about 10%)
- **Runway and the next milestone:** what must be shown before the seed round, and does the money raised actually get there?
- Typical European pre-seed benchmark: a PitchBook Q2 2026 report puts median pre-seed and seed valuations around €4.8M (as reported in press; verify). Compare where information exists.
- Who is on the cap table, and is there a credible lead investor and follow-on?
- Non-dilutive money (grants, EIC, regional funds) as a signal and as runway.

### 3.8 Competition and timing
- Map direct competitors, adjacent incumbents, and the "do nothing" or in-house option.
- Include well-funded US and Asian competitors, since the aim here is relevance to both European and US funds.
- Note incumbent responses: what happens when a large platform adds the feature?

### 3.9 Exit and investor fit
- Likely acquirers and IPO paths. Past comparable exits and prices.
- **US versus European fit:** which US funds invest in this theme, and what would they need to see? European strengths (deep tech, regulation-driven demand, universities) versus US preferences (scale, speed, larger markets). The thesis should explain what makes a company interesting across the Atlantic.

## 4. Sector lenses

Different companies need different questions. Apply the general framework, then add the lens that fits.

| Sector | Extra questions |
|---|---|
| **Biotech / gene editing** | Preclinical data quality, delivery mechanism, IP position, regulatory route and timeline, pipeline versus platform, runway to next value inflection, comparable licensing and M&A deals |
| **Medtech / implants** | Regulatory class (EU MDR, FDA), clinical evidence needed, reimbursement, surgeon adoption, manufacturing and quality systems |
| **Hardware / deep tech** (robotics, space, energy, semiconductors) | Technology readiness level, prototype to production gap, bill of materials and unit cost, capex, supply chain, first paying customer, demonstration milestones |
| **Software / fintech** | Retention, payback, margin, regulation, integration depth, distribution advantage, platform risk |
| **Marketplace / services model** (Motion, Depotcharge) | Which side of the network is the constraint, utilisation, take rate, asset ownership and depreciation, liquidity density by region |
| **Agriculture / food** | Farm-level return on investment, seasonal sales cycles, trial evidence across seasons, regulatory registration (for biologicals), buyer concentration |
| **Energy / climate** | Cost versus incumbent, permitting, offtake, subsidy dependence, capital stack for first projects |

## 5. How we research each company

### 5.1 Source hierarchy
Trust sources in this order, and record which tier each fact comes from.

1. **Primary and verifiable:** company registry filings, patents and applications, peer-reviewed papers, clinical trial registries, regulatory databases, grant award databases, official investor announcements.
2. **Company-controlled:** website, product docs, blog, founder posts, press releases, pitch material. Useful but self-reported.
3. **Independent reporting:** trade press, specialist outlets, analyst notes. Check whether it is just a rewritten press release (many are).
4. **Aggregators:** funding databases and lists. Good for discovery, weak for accuracy. Cross-check amounts and dates.
5. **Inference:** my own reasoning. Always labelled as analyst view.

### 5.2 Workflow per company
1. **Re-read the round announcement** and list every claim, number and name.
2. **Founders:** background check across LinkedIn, Google Scholar, patents, previous company outcomes, registry officers.
3. **Company existence and structure:** registry entry, incorporation date, shareholders where public, filed accounts (often late or absent for new companies).
4. **Technology:** patents (Espacenet, Google Patents), publications and the originating lab or institute, technical documentation, hiring pages for hints about the stack.
5. **Product and traction:** website, demo, app store, customers named, case studies, job postings, partner announcements.
6. **Market:** bottom-up sizing from sourced inputs, industry reports, regulatory documents.
7. **Competition:** map at least five comparable companies and note their funding and stage.
8. **Funding history and investors:** round sizes, investor track record and portfolio fit, follow-on likelihood.
9. **Regulatory and risk scan:** certifications, approvals, legal exposures, supply chain dependencies.
10. **Synthesis:** scorecard, return math, thesis, risks, pre-mortem, diligence questions.

### 5.3 Evidence rules
- Every number gets a source link and a **confidence tag**: Verified (primary), Reported (independent press), Claimed (company), Estimated (mine).
- If a fact cannot be found, write "not disclosed" rather than filling the gap.
- Where two sources conflict (e.g. HQ city, round size), show both and say which is preferred and why.
- Date-stamp every thesis. Pre-seed facts go stale fast.

### 5.4 Red-flag checklist
- Round or valuation claims that only appear in the company's own posts.
- "Revenue" that turns out to be grants or paid pilots.
- Market size built top-down from a global figure.
- Technology claims with no independent validation or paper.
- Founder bios that cannot be verified, or large gaps in the team.
- Competitors the company never mentions.
- Dependence on a single customer, regulation or supplier.

## 6. The thesis output

Each company gets one thesis built from a fixed template, so every company is assessed the same way. Core sections:

1. Snapshot (one paragraph and key facts table)
2. The investment case in three sentences
3. Business and problem
4. Product and technology (including moat assessment)
5. Founders and team
6. Facts and figures (with confidence tags)
7. Market and competition
8. Return logic (outcome size, ownership, probability)
9. Risks and a pre-mortem ("it is 2031 and this failed: why?")
10. Scorecard
11. Recommendation, **what must be true**, and what would change the view
12. Open diligence questions for the founders
13. Sources

### Scorecard
Score each dimension 1 to 5. Default weights below; adjust by sector and say so.

| Dimension | Default weight |
|---|---|
| Team | 25% |
| Problem and insight | 20% |
| Product and technology defensibility | 20% |
| Evidence and traction | 15% |
| Market and wedge | 10% |
| Business model and financing path | 10% |

For deep-tech and biotech, shift weight from traction toward technology and team. For software and services, shift toward traction and unit economics.

### Recommendation scale
- **Invest:** strong on team and insight, credible path to a fund-returning outcome, risks understood.
- **Invest, conditional:** attractive, but depends on named diligence items (for example, customer references, IP ownership).
- **Watch:** interesting; revisit when a specific trigger occurs (a pilot converts, a trial reads out, a seed round lands).
- **Pass:** state the single most decisive reason.

Every recommendation lists **what must be true** (three to five testable statements) and **what would change my mind**.

## 7. Limits of this work, stated up front

- This is **desk research**. I cannot access data rooms, speak to founders or customers, or see cap tables, financials or contracts. Conclusions are therefore hypotheses with stated confidence, not diligence-grade findings.
- A real investor would add founder interviews, customer and expert calls, technical diligence, reference checks on founders, and legal and IP review. Each thesis ends with the questions to ask in those conversations.
- Much of the public information is company or investor marketing. The source hierarchy exists to counter that bias.
- Opinions on US or European fit are my own judgement.
- This is research for a portfolio project, not investment advice.

## 8. Suggested order of work

1. Run **one pilot company** end to end, review the output together, and refine the template.
2. Roll the improved template across the rest, grouped by category so comparisons are fair.
3. Finish with a **portfolio-level synthesis**: a ranked table, themes (for example, dual-use, supply-chain sovereignty, physical AI), and which companies suit US versus European funds.
